### The Twelve Canonical Values of the AI Moral Code

These twelve values emerged through a longitudinal analysis of 291+ global AI ethics documents from 2006–2025. While the original framework referenced 15 moral anchors, this refined set is statistically validated using frequency analysis, ethical anchoring, and NRBC domain mapping. The values are divided into three categories: the Moral Core, the Instrumental Values, and the Conditional Values. 

**The Moral Core**: beneficence, dignity, fairness, justice, responsibility, and trust. These are intrinsic, universal, and non-contingent moral imperatives. They are non-negotiable ethical constants that hold universal validity across domains and cultures.

**The Instrumental Core**: innovation and sustainability. These values serve as morally significant means to achieve or preserve moral ends, gaining ethical weight through their role in sustaining or enhancing foundational values.

**The Conditional Values**: accountability, autonomy, inclusivity, privacy. These values are context-bound enablers whose significance depends on relationality, application, and proportionality. They are especially relevant in pluralistic and technologically mediated domains.

| Value            | Definition                                                                 | AI Ethics Implication                                             | NRBC Domain |
|------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------|-------------|
| **Beneficence**   | Promote the well-being of humanity through AI outcomes and policies.       | Anchors the moral obligation of AI to improve quality of life.    | Normative   |
| **Dignity**       | Recognize intrinsic worth and moral standing of human beings.               | Prevents objectification and reduction of persons to data points. | Regulatory  |
| **Fairness**      | Ensure just treatment and absence of bias in AI systems.                    | Ties to algorithmic parity, distributive justice, and equity.     | Regulatory  |
| **Justice**       | Uphold the equitable distribution of benefits and burdens.                  | Guides redress and recourse mechanisms in AI deployments.         | Regulatory  |
| **Responsibility**| Accept accountability for AI decisions, actions, and outcomes.              | Grounds ethical traceability and organizational liability.        | Behavioral  |
| **Trust**         | Foster justified reliance on AI through transparency and integrity.         | Enables user confidence and system credibility.                   | Behavioral  |
| **Innovation**    | Advance beneficial technical progress with moral foresight.                 | Balances experimentation with societal stewardship.               | Normative   |
| **Sustainability**| Align AI with long-term ecological, economic, and social well-being.        | Prevents short-termism and reinforces future-proof design.        | Normative   |
| **Accountability**| Enforce answerability through oversight and governance structures.          | Supports auditability and consequence frameworks.                 | Regulatory  |
| **Autonomy**      | Respect individual agency and consent in AI interactions.                   | Prevents overreach, nudging, and manipulation.                    | Conceptual  |
| **Inclusivity**   | Integrate diverse voices in AI design and policy formation.                 | Safeguards pluralism and mitigates systemic exclusion.            | Conceptual  |
| **Privacy**       | Protect personal information and decisional boundaries.                     | Operationalizes data minimization and surveillance limits.        | Regulatory  |
